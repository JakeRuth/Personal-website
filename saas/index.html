Field notes

Moments that shaped the engineer.

Release notes, not resume bullets. The stories that come up in real conversations.

origin 2010 · senior year

The AP CS switch

Needed a math credit. Refused AP Calc. Mrs. Nalbandian mentioned she taught AP Computer Science. I asked if it counted. She said, begrudgingly, yes. She ran a function on the board in the first five minutes of the first class. I knew right there. Fifteen years later, that sideways pivot is still the main road.

trench Summers 2012, 2013 · NYC

The moving-van summers

Two summers as a mover at Barr Brothers Moving Company in NYC, between freshman/sophomore and sophomore/junior year of college. Five to six days a week, up to 80 hours, 100% humidity in the sun. You learn fast that you can't drop a heavy thing even when your hands are slipping, because it isn't yours. Set the work-ethic floor and made the case for college sharper; the not-dropping lesson has cashed in on engineering work plenty since.

trench CommerceHub · intern era

The production takedown

Admin access to our Apache Solr cluster as an intern. Thought I was in dev; cleared a Solr core. Turned out I was in production. Site went down. Walked into the retrospective apologizing before anyone spoke. The manager stood up: "That wasn't your fault, you shouldn't have had access to that tool. The system was the problem, not you." Rewired how I think about systems, access, and blameless culture.

college 2013–2015 · UAlbany

ACM revival & Tekkapalooza

Outgoing seniors handed me a dying CS club. I got professors to let me announce an interest meeting at the end of class, booked a lecture hall, ~50–100 students showed, a few dozen joined. Ran weekly meetings, LAN parties, workshops. Organized a field trip to Tekkapalooza on behalf of the ACM club, a regional Albany-area tech showcase. Told members to bring resumes. Handed mine to CommerceHub directly. Three years of internship + a full-time role grew out of one resume drop at a non-recruiting event.

first startup Late 2014 – Nov 2016

Youni

Co-founded a college-localized social iOS app with two UAlbany D1 soccer players (Anthony, CEO; Jordan). React Native v0.13/0.14 out of free accelerator space in Troy. Anthony and Jordan won ~$25K in equity-free grants while students. Launched at SUNY Albany and Binghamton; 70-person paid-in-credits brand-ambassador program; 20,000+ posts at peak. Started on the side late 2014 alongside CommerceHub, full-time July 2016. No business model, unsolved cold-start; folded after four to five months full-time. Eyes bigger than my stomach. The eighteen months of architecture work paid off in an unexpected place, the Oscar interview question that came right after.

interview Oscar · late 2016

Ready for the question that came

The design question: "if you were going to build a social-media app where people could follow each other, how would you architect it?" I'd just spent eighteen months building Youni. I crushed it. Years later my manager told me it was a standard rotation question, asked at random, by people who hadn't read my resume. The catch was random; the prep wasn't. The single most architecturally similar question they could have asked, asked by chance, on the day my eighteen months of work had me fully ready for it. That's what serendipity actually means: luck only lands on the prepared.

founding COVID era

The Pronk emails

Daniel Pronk spent 5–10 hours every weekend building Excel sheets for his investing YouTube content. I built a Python automation in a weekend that hit cheap financial APIs and exported the same format. Started emailing him. Commenting. Trying to give it away free. Daniel ignored me for ~6 weeks; eventually replied to get me to go away. I jumped on a video call, cracked a beer, and we hit it off. He let me into his paid Discord for free.

PMF Discord drop

The Discord drop

Daniel's paid Discord, ~1,000 members behind a YouTube channel of ~100K subscribers at the time. I dropped an early Excel-generator alpha that worked for a handful of stocks. The replies came fast: "are you selling this?" / "could you add this stock?" / "I would pay for this." That's the moment I knew there was a business in it.

personality Oscar Health talent show

The unicycle cube

Old Oscar office, culture at peak, crowd half-drunk. I'd been a competitive cuber since 2008 and a trick-grade unicyclist since junior year of high school. Hand a Rubik's cube to someone, ride out on the unicycle, switch to riding backwards, jump an obstacle course of stacked chairs and a bench, take the now-scrambled cube back, solve it on the unicycle as the finale. "All the Small Things" in the background. Did it twice (re-trained for the encore, mastery decays fast).

culture Oscar · peak culture era

Emotiqueue

Started as a goof: I kept making custom emoticons for our team chat (HipChat at the time, later Slack). That habit grew into a hackathon project where messages got routed through a deliberately ridiculous pipeline (queues into queues, into a ticketing system, back into chat) ending in a chatbot. By the time I left it had 3,000+ custom emoticons, 50+ silly commands, and a codebase other engineers across the company committed to. Half goof, half real internal tool. The kind of culture moment that's missing from most engineering orgs.
