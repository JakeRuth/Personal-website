Field notes

Moments that shaped the engineer.

Release notes, not resume bullets. The stories that come up in real conversations.

origin 2010 · senior year

The AP CS switch

Needed a math credit. Refused AP Calc. Mrs. Nalbandian mentioned she taught AP Computer Science. I asked if it counted. She said, begrudgingly, yes. She ran a function on the board in the first five minutes of the first class. I knew right there. Fifteen years later, that sideways pivot is still the main road.

trench CommerceHub · intern era

The production takedown

I had access to a tool that could take a system in production down with a single button press. I pressed it. Walked into the meeting nervous, started apologizing before anyone spoke. The manager stood up: "That wasn't your fault, you shouldn't have had access to that tool." Rewired how I think about systems, access, and blameless culture.

college 2013–2015 · UAlbany

ACM revival & Tekkapalooza

Outgoing seniors handed me a dying CS club. I got professors to let me announce an interest meeting at the end of class, booked a lecture hall, ~50–100 students showed, a few dozen joined. Ran weekly meetings, LAN parties, workshops. Organized a field trip to Tekkapalooza. Told members to bring resumes. Handed mine to CommerceHub directly. Three years of internship + a full-time role grew out of one resume drop at a non-recruiting event.

interview Oscar · late 2016

The Oscar interview luck

The design question: "if you were going to build a social-media app where people could follow each other, how would you architect it?" I'd just spent eighteen months building Youni. I crushed it. Years later my manager told me it was a standard rotation question, asked at random, by people who hadn't read my resume. The single most architecturally similar question they could have asked, asked by chance, on the day that mattered most. The moment I think about when I try to explain why I believe in serendipity.

founding COVID era

The Pronk emails

Daniel Pronk spent 5–10 hours every weekend building Excel sheets for his investing YouTube content. I built a Python automation in a weekend that hit cheap financial APIs and exported the same format. Started emailing him. Commenting. Trying to give it away free. Daniel ignored me for ~6 weeks; eventually replied to get me to go away. I jumped on a video call, cracked a beer, and we hit it off. He let me into his paid Discord for free.

PMF Discord drop

The Discord drop

Daniel's paid Discord, ~1,000 members behind a YouTube channel of ~100K subscribers at the time. I dropped an early Excel-generator alpha that worked for a handful of stocks. The replies came fast: "are you selling this?" / "could you add this stock?" / "I would pay for this." That's the moment I knew there was a business in it.

personality Oscar talent show

The unicycle cube

Old Oscar office, culture at peak, crowd half-drunk. I'd been a competitive cuber since 2008 and a trick-grade unicyclist since junior year of high school. Hand a Rubik's cube to someone, ride out on the unicycle, switch to riding backwards, jump an obstacle course of stacked chairs and a bench, take the now-scrambled cube back, solve it on the unicycle as the finale. "All the Small Things" in the background. Did it twice (re-trained for the encore, mastery decays fast).

culture Oscar · with Peter

Emotiqueue

Started as a custom-emoticon flood (HipChat, then Slack). Became a hackathon project where messages got routed through a deliberately ridiculous pipeline (queues into queues, into a ticketing system, back into chat) ending in a chatbot. By the time I left it had 3,000+ custom emoticons, 50+ silly commands, and a codebase other engineers across the company committed to. Half goof, half real internal tool. The kind of culture moment that's missing from most engineering orgs.

AI-era Stock Unlock

The Customer.io migration

Migrated us off Customer.io onto self-hosted AWS SES in roughly a focused weekend, SES integration, admin panels for broadcasts and campaign config, AWS scaffolding. Most of the code came out of Claude Code; I read the parts that mattered (security, deliverability, business logic) and shipped. Saved ~$5–6K/year. One person, one weekend. That's what AI-era engineering actually looks like when you know what you're doing.

AI-era post-Opus 4.5

The Webflow migration

Migrated the marketing site off Webflow into a self-hosted custom codebase, written almost entirely with Claude Code. Direct savings were modest (~$1K/year). The wins: no contractors needed for copy changes, full UX control, the marketing surface is now 20x bigger in content pages, programmatic SEO + GEO pages on top, all AI-generated and human-reviewed.

AI-era Stock Unlock · internal

The X poster

Self-improving AI agent that drafts X posts with a human-in-the-loop. Bootstrap-trained on thousands of posts (ours plus competitors), each tagged with real engagement metrics. Aware of every product feature. Spins up a Playwright browser, authenticates as a real user via custom test-IDs, takes in-product screenshots, error-checks them, queues drafts in an admin UI. A LangChain pipeline iteratively rewrites the drafter's own prompt from human feedback; a separate cron feeds back live engagement signals. Probably the most ambitious thing I've built that isn't a company.

AI-era spare hours

The blinds motor

First hardware project I've ever attempted. A wirelessly-controlled motor for the blinds in my apartment, Adafruit Feather, a small motor, more circuitry than I fully understood when I started, all uploaded via the Arduino IDE. Codex writes most of the embedded code; I learn enough hardware to stay in the driver's seat. Pulling off a domain I've never touched in spare hours is part of why I'm convinced the 10x → 100x shift in engineer output is real and recent.

trench CommerceHub · 2014

The IE7 ellipsis

String-truncation bug in a company-name display where the ellipsis didn't render in Internet Explorer 7. QVC was pinned to IE7. To test the fix I had to spin up a virtual machine running an old version of Windows just to load the browser. In 2014. Engineering history is preserved in QVC's procurement contracts.

Principles

What I'm for. What I'm against.

No deck. No buzzwords. The convictions actually load-bearing under the work.

for teammates

Brutally honest, no sandwiches

Brutally honest teammates who disagree directly, with reason. No compliment-sandwiches. Autonomy and real ownership. Things that matter and don't sound like bullshit. High-bar teams. Get-shit-done culture.

for AI thesis

Driver in the seat

AI as a first-class tool, with the human in the driver's seat. AI writes more code than I do; human-reviewed where it matters (business logic, security, system architecture); let loose on cosmetic frontend. Opus 4.5 in early 2026 was the inflection point. Leaning all the way in, coding, deep business strategy, drafting emails and PDFs, even hardware projects on the side. The 10x → 100x shift is real and recent.

for culture

Culture carrier by default

The people, the interactions, the personality of a team, I'm conscious of being a positive force in those wherever I'm working. Emotiqueue is one example of that energy in action.

against bureaucracy

Bureaucracy and PIP theater

Politics over shipping, PIP-driven theater, buzzword resumes ("passionate," "results-oriented," "dynamic team player"). "Fisher-Price" software, looks polished, breaks under real use.

against Stock Unlock origin

Overcharging is theft

Overcharging for software that ships people short, especially when the people getting shorted can't afford it. That conviction was load-bearing under Stock Unlock and shows up in every product call I make.

against vibes

Vibe coding sold as engineering

There's a real difference. Driver in the seat means the human still owns business logic, security, and architecture. Cosmetic frontend can run loose. The two get conflated; they shouldn't.

worldview serendipity

Luck only finds people who keep running

Most of what's worked for me looks lucky in retrospect. AP CS to dodge AP Calc. The Tekkapalooza resume drop. The Oscar interview question. Daniel replying out of exhaustion. YC reaching out via LinkedIn. The pattern is that luck only finds people who keep running at it.
