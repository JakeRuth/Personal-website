A cube-shaped transition.

v1 barely looked like it was moving. v2 uses the group-attach pattern every real Three.js cube uses: weld the nine cubies of a face into a temporary group, rotate the group ninety degrees, snap, detach. Each turn reads as a turn.

Competed 2008–2014. 13.95-second average on a 3x3. The solver is still bidirectional BFS on the cubie state — fresh solution every trigger. Only the animation changed.

You're through the cube.

The cube scrambled, solved, and shrunk out of the way. This panel was always here — it just couldn't fade in until the solve finished. In a real site this is the next experience: terminal, blueprint, whatever's next.

What v1 got wrong

Stickers lived as separate meshes from the cubie bodies. The commit step reset their rotation to (0,0,0), which flattened them against their parent every turn. The cube also had a constant spin compounding with each layer rotation. The result: jittery mess.

What v2 does instead

Each cubie is a Group with its stickers as children. Layer rotations use group.attach() / scene.attach(), which preserve world transforms across reparents. Snap to exactly ±π/2 before detaching. One move at a time.

Console logs

Open DevTools on the demo page. Every move logs with its index, axis, and angle. The 4×U identity check runs once per page load. Drift, if any, shows up in the "snap:" line.
