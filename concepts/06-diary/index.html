Chapter One

Before Code

Windows 95 hum. A Rubik’s cube with a cracked corner. A skateboard leaning on the radiator. Guitar Hero on Expert.

Childhood ran on a desktop tower that sounded like it was about to take off. Before the internet was a place you lived, it was a place you visited — dial-up handshake, a little crackle, then: everything.

I fell for toys that rewarded stubbornness. A Rubik’s cube that took three days to solve, then three weeks to shave a minute off, then three years to get to a 13.95-second average. Skateboards that punished you until they didn’t. Guitar Hero buttons worn soft.

Nothing I cared about came with instructions.

Chapter Two

The Pivot

Senior year. Scheduling conflict. AP Computer Science is the loophole out of AP Calc. I sign up to dodge derivatives. Accidentally sign up for the rest of my life.

The first program we wrote was the oldest joke in the profession. I didn’t know that. I just knew I typed something and the machine said it back.

jake@highschool ~ % 
$ javac Hello.java && java Hello
█

I went home that night and installed Java on our home desktop. My mom asked what I was doing. I said: I think this is it.

Chapter Three

Rising

CommerceHub, Albany, 2013. A class field trip to Tekkapalooza. I printed twenty resumes and handed them out like flyers at a show.

That field trip got me my first internship. Serendipity — but the kind you have to reach out and grab while it’s walking past you.

I was twenty and writing code that shipped to real customers. One afternoon I took down production. My manager looked at the incident and said, flatly, “that shouldn’t have been allowed to happen,” and took the blame for me. I have tried to be that kind of manager ever since.

Three years at CommerceHub. The meter below is — approximately — how I felt about my own code. Calibrate for ego accordingly.

Chapter Four

Oscar

2017. Manhattan. A health-insurance company that thought it was a software company. I arrive when engineering is 50 people. I leave when it’s 150+.

Oscar is where I learned that scale is mostly a communication problem wearing a code costume. I watched one engineering org become three, then seven. I watched standups turn into rituals, rituals turn into bureaucracy, bureaucracy turn into people leaving, people leaving turn into rebuilding. Four years of it.

Somewhere in there I rode a unicycle on stage at the company talent show, solving a Rubik’s cube. It remains the single most Oscar-coded thing I have ever done.

Chapter Five

Stock Unlock

I met Daniel Pronk by writing him free code. A spreadsheet automation, emailed into the void. He eventually answered. A year later we were in YC.

The whole thing started because I was annoyed. Retail investors were being charged steakhouse prices for gas-station data. I thought: surely we can do better than this, and charge less while we’re at it.

YC W22. $1.335M seed. Eight people at peak. Thousands of paying customers. Profitable. I am proud of the thing and of the restraint — we built what we said we’d build and charged what we said we’d charge.

It’s a profitable side business now. I’m not full-time. Next chapter.
